\begin{abstract}
Vision-language-action (VLA) models map vision and language to robot actions. With limited post-training demonstrations, however, they often cannot detect or recover from execution failures that move the robot out of distribution (OOD), such as mis-grasps, dropped objects, unexpected occlusions, and object or robot displacement. Existing solutions rely on external failure detectors and costly intervention or recovery data, leaving detection loosely coupled with control. We introduce DUET (Dual-manifold Unified Execution and Transfer), a dual-action-head module that predicts relative and absolute actions. DUET uses the endogenous disagreement between the two heads as its primary failure signal, complemented by state-distribution distance. The absolute head excludes the current robot state and predicts RVQ-anchored global waypoints from vision and language, providing stable recovery targets when relative control becomes unreliable. We evaluate DUET on four LIBERO suites under five families of execution failures. DUET preserves nominal success ($96.9\%$ vs.\ $93.8\%$ for the relative-only baseline) while raising recovery success from $27.9\%$ to $64.6\%$ under action-execution failures, outperforming the strongest relative-only baseline by $36.7$ percentage points; it leads all success-only detectors on AUROC ($0.845$) using the same signal that drives recovery.
\end{abstract}

\section{INTRODUCTION}

Vision-language-action (VLA) policies adapt pretrained vision-language models to map visual observations and language instructions directly to robot actions \cite{rt2,openvla,starvla}. Despite their semantic generalization, policies post-trained on limited robot demonstrations remain vulnerable to failures during closed-loop execution. We focus on failures that drive the visual observation, robot--object configuration, or induced trajectory out of distribution (OOD) relative to successful demonstrations. They include policy-induced errors, such as an off-center grasp or an accidentally dropped object, as well as external events, such as transient occlusion or unexpected displacement of an object or the robot arm. After such events, a VLA may continue to produce locally plausible actions even though its trajectory is no longer supported by the demonstrations. Detecting these failures and actively recovering from them can therefore substantially improve task success and deployment reliability.

Existing approaches commonly introduce an external monitor, critic, verifier, or predictive model for failure detection \cite{faildetect}, and rely on large amounts of human takeover data collected during failed executions to learn recovery \cite{dagger,sirius,intervengen}. This process is costly because operators must continuously monitor rollouts and provide corrective actions across diverse visual, object, and robot-state failures. Moreover, detection and recovery are learned separately, so a failure alarm is not necessarily aligned with the action needed to recover. We instead seek a single policy that uses endogenous action information to detect and recover from OOD failures without additional failure or intervention data.

DUET takes disagreement between different action spaces as an endogenous signal for failure detection, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}. In our preliminary experiments, policies trained with relative actions produced smoother and more stable motions during nominal execution, but struggled to recover once a grasp or motion deviated because state-relative commands provide no explicit return target. In contrast, policies trained with absolute actions could redirect the robot toward trajectories supported by the demonstration distribution and retry after a failure. This complementary behavior motivates DUET to learn the same demonstrated behavior with a relative action head and an absolute action head. The relative head serves as the nominal controller, while the absolute head provides global recovery anchors. The two action spaces agree under in-domain execution but necessarily diverge when a failure or OOD event drives the robot away from the demonstrated trajectory, thereby coupling failure detection and recovery through the same signal.

At inference time, DUET converts the predicted absolute waypoint into a correction from the live pose and compares it with the relative action. The two predictions remain consistent along successful trajectories but diverge when a failure drives execution OOD. Their disagreement, together with distance from the training state distribution, determines a soft fusion weight: DUET follows the smooth relative controller during nominal execution, shifts toward the absolute recovery target after failure, and returns to relative control after re-entering the demonstrated support. The same cross-space signal therefore both detects failure and determines the recovery direction.

Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose DUET, a dual-action-head VLA that learns relative and absolute action spaces from successful demonstrations and uses their endogenous disagreement to detect OOD-induced failures without an external detector.
    \item We design a stateless, RVQ-anchored absolute action head and an adaptive closed-loop controller that converts cross-space disagreement into stable recovery while preserving precise relative control under nominal conditions.
    \item We validate DUET on diverse simulated and real-world failures, including mis-grasps, object drops, occlusions, and unexpected displacements.
\end{itemize}

\section{RELATED WORK}

\subsection{Failure Detection for Robot Policies}
Runtime failure detection estimates whether a policy remains within the support of successful behavior. Diff-DAgger uses diffusion uncertainty to request expert intervention \cite{diffdagger}, while Sentinel monitors action inconsistency and task progress \cite{sentinel}. FAIL-Detect formulates failure detection as sequential OOD testing using only successful executions \cite{faildetect}; FIPER similarly combines representation novelty with action uncertainty without failure data \cite{fiper}.

VLA-specific methods use internal features or external foundation models. SAFE learns a detector from successful and failed rollouts \cite{safe}, whereas AHA trains a VLM to detect and diagnose failures \cite{aha}. Such methods require additional supervision or a separate detector whose output is not a recovery action. DUET instead detects failures through disagreement between two action spaces learned within the policy, using OOD manifold inconsistency as both an alarm and a recovery signal.

\subsection{Recovery from Execution Failures}
Recovery methods commonly expand the training distribution with expert corrections. DAgger queries experts on policy-induced states \cite{dagger}; intervention-based methods collect human takeovers \cite{eil,sirius}; and IntervenGen transforms limited interventions into corrective trajectories \cite{intervengen}. These approaches reduce compounding errors but still require additional recovery actions.

Foundation-model systems instead use semantic supervisors. RACER learns from language-annotated recovery trajectories \cite{racer}, while AHA and VLM controllers diagnose failures and guide downstream correction \cite{aha,vlmrecovery}. Their detection and recovery components remain separately trained or prompted. DUET couples both capabilities without extra recovery data: absolute states from successful demonstrations provide recovery anchors, while cross-space disagreement determines when and how strongly to move toward them.

\section{METHOD}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
At time $t$, the robot observes multi-view images $o_t$, a language instruction $l$, and its current proprioceptive state $s_t$. DUET is trained only on successful demonstrations and predicts the demonstrated behavior in two action representations over a horizon $K$:
\begin{align}
    \hat{\Delta A}_t &= f_{\mathrm{rel}}(o_t,l,s_t)
    \in \mathbb{R}^{K\times 7}, \\
    \hat{W}_t &= f_{\mathrm{abs}}(o_t,l)
    \in \mathbb{R}^{K\times 7}.
\end{align}
Here, $\Delta A_t$ contains relative operational-space-control commands and $W_t$ contains future absolute end-effector waypoints $[x,y,z,r_x,r_y,r_z,g]$. We decompose each command as $a_t=[a_{p,t},a_{g,t}]$, where $a_{p,t}\in\mathbb{R}^6$ controls end-effector pose and $a_{g,t}\in\mathbb{R}$ controls the gripper. A successful trajectory naturally supplies both labels: its recorded delta actions supervise $f_{\mathrm{rel}}$, while its future end-effector states supervise $f_{\mathrm{abs}}$. The geometric analysis and fusion operate on $a_p$; although the absolute head also predicts gripper state during training, the deployed $a_g$ always comes from the relative head.

\subsection{Dual Action Manifolds}
\label{sec:manifolds}
For an instruction $l$, let $\mathcal{M}_l\subset SE(3)$ denote the end-effector state manifold traced by successful demonstrations, and let $T\mathcal{M}_l$ denote its tangent bundle. Under nominal execution, the relative head predicts a local motion vector
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\delta a}_{p,t} = f_{\mathrm{rel}}(o_t,l,s_t)_p
    \in T_{p_t}\mathcal{M}_l,
\end{equation}
where $p_t$ is the current end-effector pose. This tangent representation is locally smooth and expressive, but it specifies only how to move from the current chart. Once a failure displaces the robot from the demonstrated support, the extrapolated vector field contains no explicit destination to which the robot should return.

The absolute head instead predicts a future coordinate
\begin{equation}
    \hat{w}_{p,t}=f_{\mathrm{abs}}(o_t,l)_p\in\mathcal{M}_l,
\end{equation}
which acts as a visual-language-conditioned global anchor. To compare the two representations, we introduce a state-dependent transport map
\begin{equation}
    c_t=\Psi_{p_t}(\hat{w}_{p,t})\in T_{p_t}SE(3),
    \label{eq:transport_map}
\end{equation}
which converts the absolute waypoint into a local correction at the live pose. Along an in-domain trajectory, $c_t$ and $\hat{\delta a}_{p,t}$ describe the same demonstrated motion in two coordinate systems and should therefore align. When a failure drives execution OOD, the relative head extrapolates a local field while the absolute head remains anchored to a demonstrated destination, exposing a cross-space inconsistency.

This geometry couples detection and recovery without asserting a formal OOD guarantee: the norm of the inconsistency provides a scalar failure signal, whereas the transported vector $c_t$ already provides the corresponding recovery direction. DUET can therefore change both when and where the controller moves using the same pair of predictions.

\subsection{Dual-Head Architecture}
Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the shared VLM and two flow-matching action heads \cite{diffusionpolicy,pi0}. The relative head attends to the full hidden sequence and explicitly receives $s_t$ through a numerical state encoder. It is therefore state-aware and serves as the primary controller during nominal execution. The absolute head receives no explicit current proprioception. When state tokens are appended to the causal VLM prompt, they are placed last and excluded from absolute-branch pooling, while the relative head can still attend to them. This separation prevents the absolute predictor from collapsing into an integration of the current state and preserves its role as a global reference.

The absolute branch pools its state-free hidden sequence $H^0$ and applies residual vector quantization (RVQ) \cite{vqvae,rvq}:
\begin{equation}
    Z_q=\operatorname{RVQ}\!\left(\operatorname{Pool}(H^0)\right).
\end{equation}
RVQ maps continuous conditions to a discrete atlas learned from successful demonstrations, anchoring absolute predictions to recurrent visual-language modes rather than an unconstrained feature vector. Its residual norm is retained only as auxiliary telemetry and is not part of the primary failure score. The absolute flow head additionally uses classifier-free guidance (CFG) \cite{cfg}, enabling its conditional prediction to be softened toward the demonstrated waypoint marginal during recovery.

\subsection{Disagreement-Preserving Learning}
\label{sec:learning}
The two heads are supervised by the paired labels available in every successful trajectory. Both use flow-matching objectives, while RVQ contributes a commitment loss. The complete default objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} =
    \lambda_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rel}}
    + \lambda_{\mathrm{abs}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{abs}}
    + \lambda_{\mathrm{vq}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{vq}},
\label{eq:training_loss}
\end{equation}
where the absolute labels are normalized future states and the relative labels are the recorded delta commands. We deliberately impose no cross-head consistency loss or distillation: forcing agreement everywhere would suppress the inconsistency needed for failure detection.

DUET instead specializes the branches through asymmetric visual augmentation. The absolute head and RVQ adapter train on both clean and perturbed observations, encouraging the global anchor to remain stable under degraded input. The relative head is optimized only on the clean subset $\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{clean}}$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rel}} =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{clean}}|}
    \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{clean}}}
    \ell_{\mathrm{FM}}(\hat{\Delta a}^{(i)}, \Delta a^{(i)}).
\end{equation}
This gradient truncation preserves relative-head sensitivity while training the absolute branch as the robust reference, making cross-space disagreement more discriminative under visual OOD. For CFG training, the absolute head also replaces its quantized condition with learned null tokens on a subset of samples and learns both conditional and marginal waypoint flows.

\subsection{Joint Failure Detection and Recovery}
At deployment, the policy returns both unnormalized action chunks. Let the live pose be $p_t=(x_t,R_t)$ and the pose component of the current absolute waypoint be $\hat{w}_{p,t}=(\hat{x}_t,\hat{R}_t)$. DUET implements the transport map in Eq.~\eqref{eq:transport_map} as
\begin{equation}
    c_t=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
    \begin{bmatrix}
    (\hat{x}_t-x_t)/\sigma_p \\
    \operatorname{Log}(\hat{R}_tR_t^{-1})/\sigma_R
    \end{bmatrix},-1,1\right),
    \label{eq:recovery_vector}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{Log}:SO(3)\rightarrow\mathfrak{so}(3)$ gives the geodesic rotation correction and $\sigma_p,\sigma_R$ convert pose error into action units. The cross-space disagreement is
\begin{equation}
    D_t=\frac{\left\|c_t-
    \operatorname{clip}(\hat{\delta a}_{p,t},-1,1)\right\|_2}{\sqrt{6}}.
    \label{eq:disagreement}
\end{equation}
This is the primary failure signal. We supplement it with a coarse state-distance term that measures normalized violation of the training-state bounds:
\begin{equation}
    S_t =
    \mathrm{mean}\left[
    \frac{\max(s_{\min} - p_t, 0)}{s_{\max}-s_{\min}}
    +
    \frac{\max(p_t - s_{\max}, 0)}{s_{\max}-s_{\min}}
    \right].
\end{equation}
The resulting failure score and recovery weight are
\begin{align}
    q_t &= D_t + \lambda S_t, \\
    \alpha_t &= \sigma(k(q_t-\tau)).
\end{align}
DUET then interpolates between local execution and global recovery while retaining relative gripper control:
\begin{align}
    a_t &= [a_{p,t},a_{g,t}], \\
    a_{p,t} &= (1-\alpha_t)\hat{\delta a}_{p,t} + \alpha_t c_t, \\
    a_{g,t} &= \hat{\delta a}_{g,t},
    \label{eq:fused_action}
\end{align}
where $[\hat{\delta a}_{p,t},\hat{\delta a}_{g,t}]$ is the relative-head command. Thus relative control dominates along the demonstrated support, while a rising failure score shifts $a_p$ toward the absolute recovery direction; control returns smoothly after the two representations realign. When requesting a new chunk, the previous fusion weight also softens the absolute-head CFG scale,
\begin{equation}
    w_t = w_{\max} - (w_{\max}-w_{\min})\alpha_{t-1}.
\end{equation}
The disagreement $D_t$ therefore determines \emph{when} recovery is needed, while the vector $c_t$ determines \emph{how} to recover.

\section{EXPERIMENTS}
\label{sec:experiments}

The experiments follow the three contributions in order. \S\ref{sec:detection} tests whether cross-space disagreement separates OOD from nominal execution without any failure labels. \S\ref{sec:manifold} tests the geometric premise of \S\ref{sec:manifolds}: that the stateless absolute head actually contracts distance to the demonstrated manifold, which the relative head cannot do on its own. \S\ref{sec:main} measures whether the adaptive gate converts that geometry into task success without sacrificing nominal control, and \S\ref{sec:ablations} isolates the components responsible.

\subsection{Benchmark and Data}
\label{sec:benchmark}
We evaluate on four LIBERO suites \cite{libero}---Spatial, Object, Goal, and LIBERO-10---with $10$ tasks and $50$ initial states per task, i.e.\ $500$ episodes per suite and $2{,}000$ episodes per protocol. Data comes from the StarVLA LeRobot pipeline. The dual-label configuration uses current plus future state indices $0,\ldots,8$ for absolute waypoints and the native delta OSC commands for relative labels, so every successful demonstration supplies both supervisions without additional annotation. We report suite-specific $10$k-step checkpoints.

Four controllers share a single checkpoint and differ only at inference. \emph{DUET-Rel} clamps $\alpha_t{=}0$ and therefore isolates dual-head co-training from fusion; \emph{DUET} runs the full adaptive controller of Eq.~\eqref{eq:fused_action}; absolute-only ($\alpha_t{=}1$) and fixed-fusion ($\alpha_t{=}0.5$) variants bracket the gate. As an external reference we evaluate the original relative-only StarVLA \cite{starvla} checkpoint under the identical fixed-step protocol. The chain StarVLA $\to$ DUET-Rel therefore measures co-training, and DUET-Rel $\to$ DUET isolates the recovery mechanism itself.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:impl}
DUET is implemented as \texttt{QwenGR00THybrid} in StarVLA. The backbone is Qwen-VL with hidden dimension $2048$. The relative branch uses a 16-layer DiT-B flow-matching head; the absolute branch uses an 8-layer DiT-B flow-matching head with $16$ RVQ condition tokens of output dimension $1024$. RVQ uses three residual quantizers with codebook size $512$ and code dimension $256$. We use visual augmentation probability $p_{\mathrm{aug}}=0.3$, CFG condition-dropout probability $p_{\mathrm{drop}}=0.15$, and disable the optional learned gate by default. Training uses AdamW. The closed-loop gate uses $\tau=0.5$, $k=8$, and $\lambda=1$; \S\ref{sec:ablations} shows these sit inside a broad plateau.

\subsection{Failure Injection Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
We evaluate five failure families that each drive a different quantity out of distribution, spanning both policy-induced and externally induced events (Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy}); Fig.~\ref{fig:showcase} shows what each looks like in a rollout. Perturbations are scheduled at a fixed policy step with deterministic seeds, and DUET and DUET-Rel episodes are paired by identical $(\mathrm{task},\mathrm{initial\ state})$ keys, so every $\Delta$ below is a paired comparison reported with McNemar $p$-values. When a protocol carries a semantic precondition---grasp drop requires a held object---we report injection coverage and retain uninjected episodes rather than discarding them. We log action traces, cross-space disagreement $D_t$, state distance $S_t$, fusion weight $\alpha_t$, CFG scale $w_t$, and distance to the demonstrated manifold at every step. Because heterogeneous disturbances are not commensurable, we never pool them into a single average.

DUET leads on TPR ($0.900$) and AUROC ($0.845$), the threshold-free ranking metric and therefore the appropriate primary measure here: unlike an external monitor, DUET never thresholds its score into a binary alarm but feeds it through a continuous $\alpha_t\in[0,1]$. The $38.3$-step delay likewise reflects the step at which a fixed threshold is crossed, not when correction begins.

\subsection{Dual-Manifold Geometry of Recovery}
\label{sec:manifold}
The claim of \S\ref{sec:manifolds} is geometric: the absolute head should pull execution back toward $\mathcal{M}_l$, while the relative head, lacking a return target, should merely extrapolate. We test this directly by measuring $d_{\mathcal{M}}$, the distance from the live end-effector pose to the nearest point on $50$ clean reference trajectories for the same task, under EEF teleport on the Object suite ($n{=}484$--$498$ injected episodes per controller).

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_manifold_distance.pdf}

Left: distance $d_{\mathcal{M}}$ to the demonstration manifold at the measured post-injection horizons, means with bootstrap 95\% CIs; the dashed line marks injection. All controllers are displaced by $\approx\!7$\,cm, but only adaptive fusion contracts the distance monotonically. Right: manifold re-entry rate against task success. \label{fig:manifold}

Figure~\ref{fig:manifold} (left) shows three distinct signatures. All controllers are displaced by roughly the same amount ($d_0=7.2$, $6.4$, and $7.1$\,cm for relative-only, absolute-only, and DUET). Thirty-two steps later, relative-only control has closed only $0.71$\,cm of that gap and re-enters the demonstrated tube in $62.5\%$ of episodes with a median latency of $58$ steps: the tangent field is locally plausible but has no destination. DUET closes $4.69$\,cm, re-enters in $97.9\%$ of episodes with median latency $16$ steps, and the paired per-episode difference against relative-only is $-3.98$\,cm (95\% CI $[-4.41,-3.56]$, $n{=}484$)---a $6.6\times$ larger contraction on identical initial states.

Absolute-only control is the informative failure case, and the right panel isolates it. It re-enters the tube most reliably of all three ($99.6\%$, median $11$ steps) yet completes $0\%$ of these episodes; it is also the only controller that sits off-manifold before anything is injected ($\approx\!4.5$\,cm during nominal execution, against $\approx\!0.3$\,cm for the two controllers that use the relative head). Global waypoints are therefore enough to find the manifold and not enough to act on it. The two heads are complementary rather than redundant, which is exactly why the gate must interpolate rather than switch. Figure~\ref{fig:telemetry} shows the corresponding controller dynamics: $\alpha_t$ rises as the arm drifts off-manifold, drives the absolute correction, then decays as the two heads realign.

\label{sec:main}

Even without an injected disturbance, rollouts drift from the demonstrated support, and the absolute waypoint provides a return target the relative-only controller lacks.

\paragraph{The adaptive gate is what makes the dual head usable.}
Table~\ref{tab:main_results} compares the four controllers of \S\ref{sec:benchmark} on one checkpoint; because the static-gate controls were swept on Object only, we list them against the matched Object rates rather than the four-suite averages. The two static endpoints of the gate both fail, in opposite ways. Absolute-only ($\alpha_t{=}1$) collapses to $5.2\%$ clean success against a matched $99.2\%$: as \S\ref{sec:manifold} showed, it reaches the manifold but cannot execute on it. Fixed fusion ($\alpha_t{=}0.5$) is far worse than either relative-only or DUET under clean conditions ($46.2\%$ vs.\ $99.2\%$ for both) because it corrupts precise local control at every step, and it is equally far from DUET under disturbance ($41.0$ vs.\ $84.4\%$ on action execution, $39.4$ vs.\ $76.0\%$ on displacement) because half-strength correction is too weak when recovery is actually needed. Only the disagreement-driven gate is simultaneously good at both, which is the central quantitative claim of the paper.

\paragraph{Recovery gains hold across families and suites.}
Table~\ref{tab:ood_detailed} breaks the result down per suite and severity, and Fig.~\ref{fig:overall} aggregates the paired gain per family. DUET matches or exceeds both relative-only baselines in every aggregate, and the size of the gain tracks how badly a return target is needed.

\emph{Action execution.} Corrupting the executed 6D command leaves the scene untouched and is therefore the canonical case where relative control has nothing to return to. Over $4{,}000$ paired episodes DUET reaches $64.6\%$ against $27.9\%$ for DUET-Rel ($+36.7$ pp). The gap widens with severity: DUET-Rel falls to $5.4$--$12.8\%$ under severe perturbation while DUET retains $43.2$--$60.6\%$.

\emph{Robot displacement.} Teleporting the end effector improves success from $40.0\%$ to $61.7\%$ at medium severity ($+21.7$ pp) and from $28.9\%$ to $57.1\%$ at severe ($+28.2$ pp), with Object the largest cell in both ($+39.8$ and $+47.6$ pp). Across both severities DUET reaches $59.4\%$ versus $34.5\%$ for DUET-Rel and $37.6\%$ for the original StarVLA checkpoint; the two relative-only controllers are close, so the gain is not an artifact of co-training.

\emph{Object and grasp.} At step $67$ the evaluator opens the gripper and lowers the held object by $5$\,cm for $15$ steps. On Object, DUET achieves $95.2\%$ versus $89.2\%$ for DUET-Rel ($+6.0$ pp, $p{=}2.4{\times}10^{-5}$, coverage $\ge\!98.8\%$). The margin is smaller only because both relative-only controllers already exceed $89\%$.

\emph{Visual occlusion.} A complete camera blackout removes the observation, and DUET holds the last reliable absolute waypoint as short-term memory. Over $4{,}000$ paired episodes DUET achieves $90.8\%$ versus $73.5\%$ for DUET-Rel ($+17.3$ pp) and $58.1\%$ for StarVLA; Fig.~\ref{fig:telemetry}(d) shows waypoint-guided execution during the blackout and smooth resumption afterward.

\emph{Contact and scene.} External contact forces during manipulation ($4{,}000$ paired episodes) yield the smallest margin, $95.95\%$ versus $92.22\%$ ($+3.73$ pp), since DUET-Rel already exceeds $96\%$ on two suites. The direction is uniformly non-negative, confirming that adaptive fusion does not destabilize contact-rich tasks.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:ablations}
Table~\ref{tab:ablations} separates inference-time controls from training-time replacements. Two results are decisive. First, replacing RVQ with an MLP condition adapter drives LIBERO-10 success to $0.0\%$ on clean, displacement, and action-execution protocols alike: without a discrete atlas learned from successful demonstrations, the absolute branch produces waypoints that are unusable rather than merely imprecise, so discrete anchoring is a prerequisite and not a refinement. Second, training the relative head on the same perturbed observations as the absolute branch (symmetric augmentation, i.e.\ removing the gradient truncation of \S\ref{sec:learning}) lowers clean success from $91.8\%$ to $86.8\%$ and slightly reduces recovery (displacement $58.6\%$ vs.\ $60.8\%$; action execution $60.9\%$ vs.\ $63.8\%$). Deliberate asymmetry, not extra robustness, is what makes the two branches complementary. Holding the CFG scale fixed instead of softening it with $\alpha_{t-1}$ is also costly against its matched Object reference: $88.2$ vs.\ $99.2\%$ clean, $52.6$ vs.\ $76.0\%$ under displacement, and $57.2$ vs.\ $84.4\%$ under action execution. Guidance adaptation is therefore a substantive part of the mechanism rather than a refinement, because a hard conditional waypoint is a poor recovery target precisely when the observation is already off-distribution.

\paragraph{Fusion weight on clean trajectories.}
The low binary TNR in Table~\ref{tab:detection} raises the question of how often the gate intervenes when nothing is wrong. Across $2{,}000$ clean episodes ($354{,}609$ steps) the mean $\alpha_t$ is $0.139$, the $95^{\mathrm{th}}$ percentile is $0.339$, and only $1.7\%$ of steps exceed $0.5$. The controller therefore behaves as an essentially relative policy under nominal conditions, and the residual blending is the micro-stabilization that yields the $+3.1$ pp clean gain in Table~\ref{tab:nominal}.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to $\tau$ and $k$.}
Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity} sweeps the two gate parameters on the Object suite. A broad plateau satisfies clean $>\!90\%$ and OOD $>\!75\%$ simultaneously, and our operating point $\tau{=}0.5$, $k{=}8$ sits inside it rather than at a tuned optimum. The two panels also make the trade-off visible: small $\tau$ or large $k$ triggers correction too eagerly and erodes clean success, while large $\tau$ suppresses recovery. The plateau between these regimes is wide enough that the gate does not require per-task tuning.

\section{LIMITATIONS}

DUET assumes access to absolute state labels aligned with the relative action chunks. This is natural in LIBERO and many robot logs, but may require additional calibration or state estimation on real hardware. The closed-loop gate currently uses simple min-max state statistics as a coarse proxy for distance to the demonstrated state manifold, which may be insufficient for highly multi-modal workspaces. The absolute branch is deliberately stateless; while this supports recovery, it may reduce waypoint accuracy in tasks where the current robot state contains task-relevant information not recoverable from images. Two design choices also remain unmeasured: disabling the state-to-VLM prompt injection, and replacing the analytic gate with the optional learned gate trained on synthetic state perturbations. Finally, all reported results are simulated; real-hardware validation under physical disturbances is ongoing.

\section{CONCLUSION}

We presented DUET, a dual-branch VLA policy for endogenous failure detection and recovery. By aligning a local relative-action tangent field with a stateless, RVQ-anchored absolute-waypoint manifold, DUET uses cross-space disagreement to detect when execution becomes OOD and to activate a closed-loop recovery direction. This unifies failure detection and recovery without an external detector or additional recovery demonstrations.
