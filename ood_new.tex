\begin{abstract}
Vision-language-action (VLA) models map vision and language to robot actions. With limited post-training demonstrations, however, they often cannot detect or recover from execution failures that move the robot out of distribution (OOD), such as mis-grasps, dropped objects, unexpected occlusions, and object or robot displacement. Many existing solutions rely on external failure detectors or costly intervention and recovery data, leaving detection loosely coupled with control. We introduce DUET (Dual-manifold Unified Execution and Transfer), a dual-action-head module that predicts relative and absolute actions. DUET uses the endogenous disagreement between the two heads as its primary failure signal, complemented by state-distribution distance. The absolute head excludes the current robot state and predicts RVQ-anchored global waypoints from vision and language, providing stable recovery targets when relative control becomes unreliable. We evaluate DUET on four LIBERO suites under five families of execution failures, and transfer the identical module to a $\pi_0$ backbone on a real Franka arm. DUET preserves nominal success ($96.9\%$ vs.\ $93.8\%$ for the relative-only baseline) while raising success under action-execution failures from $27.9\%$ to $64.6\%$ ($+36.7$ pp). Under our matched protocol, its disagreement score achieves the highest AUROC ($0.845$) among the evaluated success-only detectors. On hardware, DUET matches $\pi_0$ under nominal execution ($90.0\%$) and raises robot-state OOD success from $10.0\%$ to $77.5\%$.
\end{abstract}

\section{INTRODUCTION}

Vision-language-action (VLA) policies adapt pretrained vision-language models to map visual observations and language instructions directly to robot actions \cite{rt2,openvla,starvla}. Despite their semantic generalization, policies post-trained on limited robot demonstrations remain vulnerable to failures during closed-loop execution. We focus on failures that drive the visual observation, robot--object configuration, or induced trajectory out of distribution (OOD) relative to successful demonstrations. They include policy-induced errors, such as an off-center grasp or an accidentally dropped object, as well as external events, such as transient occlusion or unexpected displacement of an object or the robot arm. After such events, a VLA may continue to produce locally plausible actions even though its trajectory is no longer supported by the demonstrations. Detecting these failures and actively recovering from them can therefore substantially improve task success and deployment reliability.

Existing approaches commonly introduce an external monitor, critic, verifier, or predictive model for failure detection \cite{faildetect}, and rely on large amounts of human takeover data collected during failed executions to learn recovery \cite{dagger,sirius,intervengen}. This process is costly because operators must continuously monitor rollouts and provide corrective actions across diverse visual, object, and robot-state failures. Moreover, detection and recovery are learned separately, so a failure alarm is not necessarily aligned with the action needed to recover. We instead seek a single policy that uses endogenous action information to detect and recover from OOD failures without additional failure or intervention data.

DUET takes disagreement between different action spaces as an endogenous signal for failure detection, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}. In our preliminary experiments, policies trained with relative actions produced smoother and more stable motions during nominal execution, but struggled to recover once a grasp or motion deviated because state-relative commands provide no explicit return target. In contrast, policies trained with absolute actions could redirect the robot toward trajectories supported by the demonstration distribution and retry after a failure. This complementary behavior motivates DUET to learn the same demonstrated behavior with a relative action head and an absolute action head. The relative head serves as the nominal controller, while the absolute head provides global recovery anchors. The two action spaces agree under in-domain execution but often diverge under the execution shifts considered here, thereby coupling failure detection and recovery through the same pair of predictions.

At inference time, DUET converts the predicted absolute waypoint into a correction from the live pose and compares it with the relative action. The two predictions remain consistent along successful trajectories but diverge when a failure drives execution OOD. Their disagreement, together with distance from the training state distribution, determines a soft fusion weight: DUET follows the smooth relative controller during nominal execution, shifts toward the absolute recovery target after failure, and returns to relative control after re-entering the demonstrated support. The same cross-space signal therefore both detects failure and determines the recovery direction, except when the observation itself is invalid and a cached waypoint must be held.

Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose DUET, a dual-action-head VLA that learns relative and absolute action spaces from successful demonstrations and uses their endogenous disagreement to detect OOD-induced failures without an external detector.
    \item We design a stateless, RVQ-anchored absolute action head and an adaptive closed-loop controller that converts cross-space disagreement into stable recovery while preserving precise relative control under nominal conditions.
    \item We validate DUET on four LIBERO suites under five failure families---action-execution drift, robot displacement, grasp loss, transient occlusion, and external contact---and transfer the same module to a $\pi_0$ backbone on a real Franka arm.
\end{itemize}

\section{RELATED WORK}

\subsection{Runtime Failure Detection}
Runtime monitors seek to identify when a learned policy is unlikely to complete the task before unsafe or irreversible behavior occurs. Early interactive-learning systems treat uncertainty as a query signal: Diff-DAgger thresholds the diffusion training loss of policy-generated actions and requests expert intervention after persistent violations \cite{diffdagger}. Model-based monitoring instead rolls actions forward in a learned latent dynamics model and combines predicted OOD states with a failure classifier trained from deployment interventions \cite{liu2024runtime}. These methods can anticipate failures, but their downstream response is typically to stop or solicit a human rather than to produce an autonomous corrective action.

Recent monitors for generative policies separate different sources of failure. Sentinel detects erratic behavior through statistical temporal action consistency (STAC) and uses a VLM to assess slower task-progress failures \cite{sentinel}. FAIL-Detect fits scalar success-distribution scores, including flow-based latent likelihood, and calibrates time-varying alarms with conformal prediction using successful executions only \cite{faildetect}. FIPER similarly avoids failure data by combining representation novelty from random network distillation with action-chunk entropy; both scores are calibrated on successful rollouts and aggregated temporally \cite{fiper}. VLA-FAIL specializes success-only monitoring to fine-tuned VLAs by combining last-layer feature distance with single-sample consistency between consecutive action chunks \cite{vlafail}. Rewind-IL also monitors overlapping-chunk discrepancy, then couples its alarm to replay from VLM-selected demonstration checkpoints \cite{rewindil}. These approaches show that policy uncertainty, temporal inconsistency, and feature-space novelty are useful signals, but use an auxiliary score, cross-time comparison, or checkpoint mechanism distinct from a simultaneously predicted corrective action.

Failure detection for generalist policies increasingly exploits foundation-model representations. SAFE regresses VLA internal features to a task-generic failure score and uses conformal calibration, but trains the detector on trajectory-level successful and failed rollouts \cite{safe}. AHA instead fine-tunes a VLM on procedurally perturbed failure trajectories to generate free-form failure detection and diagnosis across tasks and embodiments \cite{aha}. Such semantic detectors can explain task-level errors that action uncertainty may miss, although they add a separately trained monitor and their textual output is not itself executable control. DUET targets a complementary regime: its detector is the geometric disagreement between two action representations trained from the same successful demonstrations. The disagreement is endogenous to the policy, and the transported absolute prediction that creates the score simultaneously supplies a continuous recovery direction.

\subsection{Learning to Recover from Failures}
A standard route to robustness is to expand the state distribution seen during training. DAgger queries an expert on learner-induced states to reduce covariate shift \cite{dagger}; intervention learning treats human takeovers as explicit or implicit corrective feedback \cite{eil}; and Sirius supports continual on-the-job improvement from deployment interventions \cite{sirius}. IntervenGen improves data efficiency by perturbing scene states around sparse human interventions and relabeling the resulting corrective trajectories \cite{intervengen}. These methods are effective when experts can demonstrate recovery, but collecting sufficiently diverse takeovers remains costly and couples robustness to anticipated failure modes.

Foundation models provide higher-level supervision for recovery. RACER automatically augments demonstrations with failure-recovery trajectories and rich language annotations, then uses a VLM supervisor to instruct a language-conditioned control policy \cite{racer}. AHA supplies natural-language diagnoses to downstream reward design, task-and-motion planning, and subtask verification systems \cite{aha}; prompt-optimized VLM controllers likewise select recovery instructions or skills from visual feedback \cite{vlmrecovery}. More directly, FailSafe generates failed trajectories paired with executable corrective actions and uses them to train VLA recovery behavior \cite{failsafe}. These approaches broaden semantic reasoning, but rely on generated or collected failure supervision, an external supervisor, or a separately interpreted recovery command.

Recent work reduces this dependence in different ways. Back to the Manifold learns a demonstration-density model and an equivariant latent action mapping that steers perturbed observations toward training support \cite{backtomanifold}; OCR derives an inverse recovery policy from an object-centric keypoint manifold \cite{ocr}. CCDP trains only on successful demonstrations but uses known failed attempts at deployment to guide diffusion sampling away from unsuccessful actions \cite{ccdp}. Rewind-IL also uses successful demonstrations without policy retraining, but recovery consists of returning to a discrete, semantically selected checkpoint \cite{rewindil}. FAR instead learns at test time from encountered failures by constructing failure-contrastive preferences, perturbing retry actions, and incorporating successful recoveries into continual improvement \cite{far}. These methods establish that recovery need not begin from human corrective demonstrations, while relying on density gradients, object-centric structure, explicit failure feedback, checkpoint restoration, or online adaptation.

DUET neither replays a stored checkpoint nor adapts from test-time failures. It learns absolute states already present in successful trajectories as observation-conditioned manifold anchors and continuously blends their transported corrections with relative control. More specifically, the same pair of policy predictions yields both the primary disagreement score and the transported correction direction; state-distance telemetry and the validity guard handle complementary cases. Detection and recovery are therefore coupled without a separate recovery policy, test-time optimizer, or failure-conditioned training set.

\subsection{Action Representations for Manipulation}
Action parameterization strongly affects policy learning and deployment. Broad comparisons across Cartesian and joint position, velocity, and torque interfaces show that action space changes exploration, constraint violation, tracking error, and sim-to-real transfer, with no single representation uniformly optimizing all properties \cite{aljalbout2024actionspace}. A large-scale study of modern manipulation policies further finds that delta actions are generally easier to learn and execute precisely than absolute targets, while noting that accumulated feedback and tracking errors can induce drift \cite{feng2026demystifying}. Absolute actions provide global geometric grounding but present a harder multimodal prediction problem.

Most policies select one representation and accept its associated trade-off. DUET instead treats relative and absolute operational-space actions as complementary views of the same demonstrated behavior. Relative actions retain well-conditioned local control; a state-free, quantized absolute head supplies global anchors. Their agreement characterizes nominal execution, while their divergence exposes states for which local motion and global destination are no longer mutually consistent. This converts an action-space design choice into a joint detection-and-recovery mechanism.

\section{METHOD}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
At time $t$, the robot observes multi-view images $o_t$, a language instruction $l$, and its current proprioceptive state $s_t$. Trained only on successful demonstrations, DUET predicts the demonstrated behavior in two action representations over a horizon $K$:
\begin{align}
    \hat{\Delta A}_t &= f_{\mathrm{rel}}(o_t,l,s_t)
    \in \mathbb{R}^{K\times 7}, \\
    \hat{W}_t &= f_{\mathrm{abs}}(o_t,l)
    \in \mathbb{R}^{K\times 7}.
\end{align}
Here, $\Delta A_t$ contains relative operational-space-control commands and $W_t$ contains future absolute end-effector waypoints $[x,y,z,r_x,r_y,r_z,g]$. Each command decomposes as $a_t=[a_{p,t},a_{g,t}]$, with pose $a_{p,t}\in\mathbb{R}^6$ and gripper $a_{g,t}\in\mathbb{R}$. A successful trajectory supplies both labels: recorded delta actions supervise $f_{\mathrm{rel}}$, and future end-effector states supervise $f_{\mathrm{abs}}$. Geometry and fusion operate on $a_p$; the absolute head predicts gripper state in training, but the deployed $a_g$ always comes from the relative head.

\subsection{Dual Action Manifolds}
\label{sec:manifolds}
For an instruction $l$, let $\mathcal{M}_l\subset SE(3)$ denote the end-effector manifold traced by successful demonstrations, and let $T\mathcal{M}_l$ denote its tangent bundle. Under nominal execution, the relative head predicts a local motion vector
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\delta a}_{p,t} = f_{\mathrm{rel}}(o_t,l,s_t)_p
    \in T_{p_t}\mathcal{M}_l,
\end{equation}
where $p_t$ is the current end-effector pose. This tangent representation is locally smooth, but specifies only how to move from the current chart. Once a failure leaves the demonstrated support, the extrapolated field has no explicit return destination.

The absolute head instead predicts a future coordinate
\begin{equation}
    \hat{w}_{p,t}=f_{\mathrm{abs}}(o_t,l)_p\in\mathcal{M}_l,
\end{equation}
which serves as a vision-language-conditioned global anchor. To compare the two representations, a state-dependent transport map
\begin{equation}
    c_t=\Psi_{p_t}(\hat{w}_{p,t})\in T_{p_t}SE(3),
    \label{eq:transport_map}
\end{equation}
converts the absolute waypoint into a local correction at the live pose. Along an in-domain trajectory, $c_t$ and $\hat{\delta a}_{p,t}$ describe the same motion in two coordinates and therefore align. When execution is driven OOD, the relative head extrapolates a local field while the absolute head remains anchored to a demonstrated destination, exposing a cross-space inconsistency.

This geometry couples detection and recovery without a formal OOD guarantee: the inconsistency norm is a scalar failure signal, and the transported vector $c_t$ is already the recovery direction. The same pair of predictions therefore decides both when and where the controller moves.

\subsection{Dual-Head Architecture}
Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the shared VLM and two flow-matching action heads \cite{diffusionpolicy,pi0}. The relative head attends to the full hidden sequence and receives $s_t$ through a numerical state encoder, making it the state-aware nominal controller. The absolute head receives no explicit proprioception. When state tokens are appended to the causal VLM prompt, they are placed last and excluded from absolute-branch pooling, while the relative head can still attend to them. This prevents the absolute predictor from collapsing into an integration of the current state and preserves it as a global reference.

The absolute branch pools its state-free hidden sequence $H^0$ and applies residual vector quantization (RVQ) \cite{vqvae,rvq}:
\begin{equation}
    Z_q=\operatorname{RVQ}\!\left(\operatorname{Pool}(H^0)\right).
\end{equation}
RVQ maps continuous conditions to a discrete atlas learned from successful demonstrations, anchoring absolute predictions to recurrent visual-language modes rather than an unconstrained feature. The residual norm is auxiliary telemetry and is not part of the primary failure score. The absolute flow head also uses classifier-free guidance (CFG) \cite{cfg}, softening the conditional prediction toward the demonstrated waypoint marginal during recovery.

\subsection{Disagreement-Preserving Learning}
\label{sec:learning}
Both heads are supervised by the paired labels in every successful trajectory, using flow-matching objectives with an RVQ commitment loss:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} =
    \lambda_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rel}}
    + \lambda_{\mathrm{abs}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{abs}}
    + \lambda_{\mathrm{vq}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{vq}},
\label{eq:training_loss}
\end{equation}
where absolute labels are normalized future states and relative labels are recorded delta commands. We impose no cross-head consistency loss or distillation: forcing agreement would suppress the inconsistency needed for detection.

The branches are instead specialized by asymmetric visual augmentation. The absolute head and RVQ adapter train on both clean and perturbed observations so that the global anchor remains stable under degraded input. The relative head is optimized only on the clean subset $\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{clean}}$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rel}} =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{clean}}|}
    \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{clean}}}
    \ell_{\mathrm{FM}}(\hat{\Delta a}^{(i)}, \Delta a^{(i)}).
\end{equation}
This gradient truncation keeps the relative head sensitive while training the absolute branch as a robust reference, making disagreement more discriminative under visual OOD. For CFG, the absolute head replaces its quantized condition with learned null tokens on a subset of samples and learns both conditional and marginal waypoint flows.

\subsection{Joint Failure Detection and Recovery}
\label{sec:method-gate}
At deployment, the policy returns both unnormalized action chunks. Let the live pose be $p_t=(x_t,R_t)$ and the pose component of the current absolute waypoint be $\hat{w}_{p,t}=(\hat{x}_t,\hat{R}_t)$. The transport map in Eq.~\eqref{eq:transport_map} is
\begin{equation}
    c_t=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
    \begin{bmatrix}
    (\hat{x}_t-x_t)/\sigma_p \\
    \operatorname{Log}(\hat{R}_tR_t^{-1})/\sigma_R
    \end{bmatrix},-1,1\right),
    \label{eq:recovery_vector}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{Log}:SO(3)\rightarrow\mathfrak{so}(3)$ is the geodesic rotation correction and $\sigma_p,\sigma_R$ convert pose error into action units. Cross-space disagreement is the primary failure signal,
\begin{equation}
    D_t=\frac{\left\|c_t-
    \operatorname{clip}(\hat{\delta a}_{p,t},-1,1)\right\|_2}{\sqrt{6}}.
    \label{eq:disagreement}
\end{equation}
We add a coarse state-distance term that measures normalized violation of the training-state bounds:
\begin{equation}
    S_t =
    \mathrm{mean}\left[
    \frac{\max(s_{\min} - p_t, 0)}{s_{\max}-s_{\min}}
    +
    \frac{\max(p_t - s_{\max}, 0)}{s_{\max}-s_{\min}}
    \right].
\end{equation}
The failure score and recovery weight are
\begin{align}
    q_t &= D_t + \lambda S_t, \\
    \alpha_t &= \sigma(k(q_t-\tau)).
\end{align}
DUET interpolates between local execution and global recovery while keeping relative gripper control:
\begin{align}
    a_t &= [a_{p,t},a_{g,t}], \\
    a_{p,t} &= (1-\alpha_t)\hat{\delta a}_{p,t} + \alpha_t c_t, \\
    a_{g,t} &= \hat{\delta a}_{g,t},
    \label{eq:fused_action}
\end{align}
where $[\hat{\delta a}_{p,t},\hat{\delta a}_{g,t}]$ is the relative-head command. Relative control therefore dominates on the demonstrated support; a rising failure score shifts $a_p$ toward the absolute recovery direction, and control returns after the two representations realign. When a new chunk is requested, the previous fusion weight also softens the absolute-head CFG scale,
\begin{equation}
    w_t = w_{\max} - (w_{\max}-w_{\min})\alpha_{t-1}.
\end{equation}
Thus $D_t$ determines \emph{when} to recover and $c_t$ determines \emph{how}. One exception is treated separately: a fully invalid observation corrupts both heads, so $D_t$ is uninformative. A validity guard then freezes the last reliable waypoint and holds $\alpha_t{=}1$ until vision returns.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
We evaluate all four LIBERO suites \cite{libero}, each containing $10$ tasks with $50$ initial states per task ($500$ episodes per suite), using suite-specific $10$k-step checkpoints. Training uses only successful demonstrations, which provide future end-effector states for the absolute head and delta OSC commands for the relative head.

We use StarVLA \cite{starvla} as the external relative-action baseline. DUET-Rel ($\alpha_t{=}0$) and DUET share the same checkpoint, so their difference isolates the effect of adaptive recovery, whereas StarVLA$\to$DUET-Rel captures the effect of dual-head co-training. Absolute-only ($\alpha_t{=}1$) and fixed fusion ($\alpha_t{=}0.5$) serve as inference-time ablations.

DUET uses Qwen-VL features (size $2048$), 16-/8-layer relative/absolute DiT-B heads, and $16$ RVQ tokens from three codebooks (size $512$, dimension $256$). We set $p_{\mathrm{aug}}{=}0.3$, $p_{\mathrm{drop}}{=}0.15$, $\lambda{=}1$, $\tau{=}0.5$, and $k{=}8$.

Perturbations are injected at fixed steps with deterministic seeds and matched $(\mathrm{task},\mathrm{initial\ state})$ keys. We retain episodes that terminate before injection, report coverage when an intervention has semantic preconditions, and assess paired success differences with McNemar tests. Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} summarizes the failure families; external force is evaluated separately, and results are not pooled across heterogeneous families.

\emph{Action execution.} Because the scene remains intact, the absolute branch provides a return target unavailable to purely relative control. Across $4{,}000$ paired episodes, DUET improves success by $36.7$ pp; under severe perturbations, it retains $43.2$--$60.6\%$ success, compared with only $5.4$--$12.8\%$ for DUET-Rel.

\emph{Robot displacement.} Teleporting the end effector moves the live pose off the demonstrated trajectory. DUET gains $24.9$ pp overall and $39.8/47.6$ pp under medium/severe Object perturbations. The Spatial differences are not significant ($p{=}0.21/0.067$), partly because some episodes finish before the step-$39$ intervention.

\emph{Object and grasp.} A $5$\,cm drop changes the robot--object relation without directly displacing the arm. DUET still improves upon the strong $89.2\%$ DUET-Rel baseline by $6.0$ pp ($p{=}2.4{\times}10^{-5}$; coverage $\geq98.8\%$), making grasp loss a harder but recoverable boundary case.

\emph{Visual occlusion.} A blank image corrupts both action heads, so recovery is driven by the validity guard rather than disagreement. DUET gains $17.3$ pp overall. Object-medium is the only regression ($-1.8$ pp, $p{>}0.05$), whereas LIBERO-10 under severe occlusion improves by $60.6$ pp.

\emph{Contact and scene.} In the separate external-force evaluation, DUET reaches $95.95\%$ success versus $92.22\%$ over $4{,}000$ episodes, with no regression on any suite.

Table~\ref{tab:real_robot} shows that DUET preserves the $90.0\%$ nominal success of $\pi_0$. Under robot-state EEF shifts, which mirror the simulation teleport protocol, the baseline falls to $10.0\%$, whereas DUET reaches $77.5\%$ ($+67.5$ pp), with gains of $65$--$70$ pp on every task. \subsection{Discussion}
The experiments both validate and delimit the proposed mechanism. Cross-space disagreement ranks failures and drives effective recovery when the live pose leaves $\mathcal{M}_l$, but it is weaker for world-state changes and uninformative when both heads receive an invalid image; the validity guard addresses the latter case. For arm-state OOD, the comparison between shared-checkpoint controllers attributes the gain to adaptive gating rather than co-training, a pattern that persists after transfer from StarVLA in simulation to $\pi_0$ on hardware. Visual dropout separates the two effects: asymmetric training improves DUET-Rel over StarVLA, while the guard adds robustness as the outage length increases. Finally, the broad $\tau$--$k$ plateau and successful backbone transfer indicate that the recovery behavior is not tied to per-suite gate tuning.

\section{LIMITATIONS}

DUET assumes absolute state labels aligned with relative action chunks---natural in LIBERO and many robot logs, but may require calibration on other hardware. The gate uses min-max state statistics as a coarse $S_t$ proxy, which may be insufficient in highly multi-modal workspaces. The stateless absolute branch supports recovery but may lose accuracy when the current robot state carries task information not visible in images. Two scope limits follow: $D_t$ is weaker on world-state change (Fig.~\ref{fig:telemetry}(b); Table~\ref{tab:real_robot}), and both heads degrade together under a fully invalid observation, leaving only the cached-waypoint guard. Irreversible failures and tasks requiring a new strategy are out of scope.

\section{CONCLUSION}

We presented DUET, a dual-branch VLA policy for endogenous failure detection and recovery. By aligning a local relative-action tangent field with a stateless, RVQ-anchored absolute-waypoint manifold, DUET uses cross-space disagreement to detect when execution becomes OOD and to activate a closed-loop recovery direction. This unifies failure detection and recovery without an external detector or additional recovery demonstrations. Because the module attaches only to the shared vision-language representation, the identical design transfers from StarVLA in simulation to $\pi_0$ on a real Franka arm without backbone-specific adaptation.
