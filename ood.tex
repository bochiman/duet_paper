\begin{abstract}
Vision-language-action (VLA) models map vision and language to robot actions. With limited post-training demonstrations, however, they often cannot detect or recover from execution failures that move the robot out of distribution (OOD), such as mis-grasps, dropped objects, unexpected occlusions, and object or robot displacement. Existing solutions rely on external failure detectors and costly intervention or recovery data, leaving detection loosely coupled with control. We introduce DUET (Dual-manifold Unified Execution and Transfer), a dual-action-head module that predicts relative and absolute actions. DUET uses the endogenous disagreement between the two heads as its primary failure signal, complemented by state-distribution distance. The absolute head excludes the current robot state and predicts RVQ-anchored global waypoints from vision and language, providing stable recovery targets when relative control becomes unreliable. We evaluate DUET in LIBERO simulation and real-world manipulation under diverse failure events. \section{INTRODUCTION}
Vision-language-action (VLA) models have recently shown strong capability for general-purpose robot manipulation \cite{rt2,kim2024openvla}. In real-world deployment, however, robots still encounter situations outside the training distribution that cascade into execution failure and limit robustness. Prior work typically organizes such generalization problems into \emph{semantic}, \emph{visual}, and \emph{behavioral} distribution shifts \cite{2026IRAL...11.3182G}.

In a typical VLA loop, a vision-language model $f_{\mathrm{VLM}}$ encodes observations, language, and robot state into task-relevant representations; an action policy then produces control commands. Most efforts to improve generalization concentrate on this first stage---enlarging the pretrained backbone, broadening vision-language pretraining, or collecting more robot and visual data---and thereby strengthen the visual-semantic representation of $f_{\mathrm{VLM}}$. Such methods can mitigate semantic OOD and much of visual OOD, but they usually incur larger models and extra data-collection cost. This raises a natural question: \emph{if the base VLM and the available training data are held fixed, where else can VLA OOD robustness still be improved?}

We argue that an underexplored source of robustness lies in how robot behavior is represented and executed. This direction is especially relevant to behavioral OOD, where the robot may already understand the task, yet its own state, the world state, or the outcome of action execution has departed from the successful behavior seen during training. For transient visual failures such as short camera blackouts or dropped frames, the action/control layer may also provide execution continuity while vision is temporarily unavailable.

Existing OOD and failure detectors typically add a monitor, verifier, or uncertainty module outside the policy, or collect extra failure and correction data \cite{liu2024runtime,agia2025sentinel,xu2025faildetect,duan2025aha,hoque2024intervengen}. Yet OOD and failure are not equivalent: an unseen state may still be handled successfully, while a policy can also fail in distribution through prediction or compounding control error. For closed-loop manipulation, a more practical question than ``has the current step already failed?'' is therefore: \emph{can the robot notice that its current execution is beginning to depart from behavior that can still complete the task?} We seek this signal endogenously from the policy's own action predictions, rather than from an extra external detector.

Action representation offers a natural entry point. Current VLA policies usually adopt a single representation, most often relative actions \cite{aljalbout2024actionspace,feng2026demystifying}. A relative action describes how to continue from the current state and is well suited to fast, fine-grained local control, but because it depends on that state it provides no persistent global reference once the robot is perturbed, an object is displaced, or execution error accumulates---and thus cannot tell whether motion is still heading toward the intended goal. An absolute action, by contrast, predicts a future end-effector pose that remains a global task-space reference and can still indicate a recovery direction after the robot state has shifted. The two representations therefore have complementary control properties.

This leads to a further question: \emph{can complementary relative and absolute actions be used jointly so that a VLA can both sense abnormal execution drift and recover from it, without modifying the base VLM or relying on extra failure--recovery data?} Doing so requires keeping the two representations complementary rather than collapsing into similar predictors; constructing a reliable execution-inconsistency signal from the two streams without OOD or failure labels; and turning that signal into continuous recovery control rather than a binary failure flag.

We propose \textbf{DUET}, a dual-action framework for robust VLA execution. DUET keeps the original vision-language backbone unchanged and jointly learns a state-aware relative stream and a state-independent absolute stream. Relative actions provide local fine-grained control under nominal conditions; absolute actions supply a global task-space anchor. Transporting each absolute prediction into the current robot frame lets DUET compare the two judgments of the current motion direction. As they diverge, the disagreement characterizes inconsistency with successful behavior and dynamically reweights relative control against absolute correction. The same pair therefore answers \emph{when} execution is becoming unreliable and \emph{where} recovery should proceed. Under transient visual failure, the most recent reliable absolute waypoint can further serve as a short-lived action reference, improving execution continuity.

We evaluate DUET under robot-state perturbations, environment/object-state changes, execution-dynamics shifts, and transient visual observation failures, testing whether the action/control layer alone can further improve VLA deployment robustness. Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item We revisit VLA OOD robustness from the action and control layer, and show that, with the base VLM and existing training data held fixed, action representation itself can strengthen robustness under behavioral OOD and transient visual failure.
\item We propose DUET, which builds complementary dual-action representations from state-aware relative actions and state-independent absolute actions. Their cross-representation disagreement provides an endogenous execution-inconsistency signal, while the absolute stream supplies the global reference needed for recovery.
\item We design an adaptive dual-action closed-loop controller and evaluate it under robot-state, world-state, execution-dynamics, and transient visual perturbations, showing that DUET improves robust execution without an extra failure detector or dedicated recovery data.
\end{itemize}

\section{RELATED WORK}

\subsection{Failure Detection for Robot Policies}
Runtime failure detection estimates whether a policy remains within the support of successful behavior. Diff-DAgger uses diffusion uncertainty to request expert intervention \cite{diffdagger}, while Sentinel monitors action inconsistency and task progress \cite{sentinel}. FAIL-Detect formulates failure detection as sequential OOD testing using only successful executions \cite{faildetect}; FIPER similarly combines representation novelty with action uncertainty without failure data \cite{fiper}.

VLA-specific methods use internal features or external foundation models. SAFE learns a detector from successful and failed rollouts \cite{safe}, whereas AHA trains a VLM to detect and diagnose failures \cite{aha}. Such methods require additional supervision or a separate detector whose output is not a recovery action. DUET instead detects failures through disagreement between two action spaces learned within the policy, using OOD manifold inconsistency as both an alarm and a recovery signal.

\subsection{Recovery from Execution Failures}
Recovery methods commonly expand the training distribution with expert corrections. DAgger queries experts on policy-induced states \cite{dagger}; intervention-based methods collect human takeovers \cite{eil,sirius}; and IntervenGen transforms limited interventions into corrective trajectories \cite{intervengen}. These approaches reduce compounding errors but still require additional recovery actions.

Foundation-model systems instead use semantic supervisors. RACER learns from language-annotated recovery trajectories \cite{racer}, while AHA and VLM controllers diagnose failures and guide downstream correction \cite{aha,vlmrecovery}. Their detection and recovery components remain separately trained or prompted. DUET couples both capabilities without extra recovery data: absolute states from successful demonstrations provide recovery anchors, while cross-space disagreement determines when and how strongly to move toward them.

\section{METHOD}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
At time $t$, the robot observes multi-view images $o_t$, a language instruction $l$, and its current proprioceptive state $s_t$. DUET is trained only on successful demonstrations and predicts the demonstrated behavior in two action representations over a horizon $K$:
\begin{align}
    \hat{\Delta A}_t &= f_{\mathrm{rel}}(o_t,l,s_t)
    \in \mathbb{R}^{K\times 7}, \\
    \hat{W}_t &= f_{\mathrm{abs}}(o_t,l)
    \in \mathbb{R}^{K\times 7}.
\end{align}
Here, $\Delta A_t$ contains relative operational-space-control commands and $W_t$ contains future absolute end-effector waypoints $[x,y,z,r_x,r_y,r_z,g]$. We decompose each command as $a_t=[a_{p,t},a_{g,t}]$, where $a_{p,t}\in\mathbb{R}^6$ controls end-effector pose and $a_{g,t}\in\mathbb{R}$ controls the gripper. A successful trajectory naturally supplies both labels: its recorded delta actions supervise $f_{\mathrm{rel}}$, while its future end-effector states supervise $f_{\mathrm{abs}}$. The geometric analysis and fusion operate on $a_p$; although the absolute head also predicts gripper state during training, the deployed $a_g$ always comes from the relative head.

\subsection{Dual Action Manifolds}
For an instruction $l$, let $\mathcal{M}_l\subset SE(3)$ denote the end-effector state manifold traced by successful demonstrations, and let $T\mathcal{M}_l$ denote its tangent bundle. Under nominal execution, the relative head predicts a local motion vector
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\delta a}_{p,t} = f_{\mathrm{rel}}(o_t,l,s_t)_p
    \in T_{p_t}\mathcal{M}_l,
\end{equation}
where $p_t$ is the current end-effector pose. This tangent representation is locally smooth and expressive, but it specifies only how to move from the current chart. Once a failure displaces the robot from the demonstrated support, the extrapolated vector field contains no explicit destination to which the robot should return.

The absolute head instead predicts a future coordinate
\begin{equation}
    \hat{w}_{p,t}=f_{\mathrm{abs}}(o_t,l)_p\in\mathcal{M}_l,
\end{equation}
which acts as a visual-language-conditioned global anchor. To compare the two representations, we introduce a state-dependent transport map
\begin{equation}
    c_t=\Psi_{p_t}(\hat{w}_{p,t})\in T_{p_t}SE(3),
    \label{eq:transport_map}
\end{equation}
which converts the absolute waypoint into a local correction at the live pose. Along an in-domain trajectory, $c_t$ and $\hat{\delta a}_{p,t}$ describe the same demonstrated motion in two coordinate systems and should therefore align. When a failure drives execution OOD, the relative head extrapolates a local field while the absolute head remains anchored to a demonstrated destination, exposing a cross-space inconsistency.

This geometry couples detection and recovery without asserting a formal OOD guarantee: the norm of the inconsistency provides a scalar failure signal, whereas the transported vector $c_t$ already provides the corresponding recovery direction. DUET can therefore change both when and where the controller moves using the same pair of predictions.

\subsection{Dual-Head Architecture}
Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the shared VLM and two flow-matching action heads \cite{diffusionpolicy,pi0}. The relative head attends to the full hidden sequence and explicitly receives $s_t$ through a numerical state encoder. It is therefore state-aware and serves as the primary controller during nominal execution. The absolute head receives no explicit current proprioception. When state tokens are appended to the causal VLM prompt, they are placed last and excluded from absolute-branch pooling, while the relative head can still attend to them. This separation prevents the absolute predictor from collapsing into an integration of the current state and preserves its role as a global reference.

The absolute branch pools its state-free hidden sequence $H^0$ and applies residual vector quantization (RVQ) \cite{vqvae,rvq}:
\begin{equation}
    Z_q=\operatorname{RVQ}\!\left(\operatorname{Pool}(H^0)\right).
\end{equation}
RVQ maps continuous conditions to a discrete atlas learned from successful demonstrations, anchoring absolute predictions to recurrent visual-language modes rather than an unconstrained feature vector. Its residual norm is retained only as auxiliary telemetry and is not part of the primary failure score. The absolute flow head additionally uses classifier-free guidance (CFG) \cite{cfg}, enabling its conditional prediction to be softened toward the demonstrated waypoint marginal during recovery.

\subsection{Disagreement-Preserving Learning}
The two heads are supervised by the paired labels available in every successful trajectory. Both use flow-matching objectives, while RVQ contributes a commitment loss. The complete default objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} =
    \lambda_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rel}}
    + \lambda_{\mathrm{abs}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{abs}}
    + \lambda_{\mathrm{vq}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{vq}},
\label{eq:training_loss}
\end{equation}
where the absolute labels are normalized future states and the relative labels are the recorded delta commands. We deliberately impose no cross-head consistency loss or distillation: forcing agreement everywhere would suppress the inconsistency needed for failure detection.

DUET instead specializes the branches through asymmetric visual augmentation. The absolute head and RVQ adapter train on both clean and perturbed observations, encouraging the global anchor to remain stable under degraded input. The relative head is optimized only on the clean subset $\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{clean}}$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rel}} =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{clean}}|}
    \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{clean}}}
    \ell_{\mathrm{FM}}(\hat{\Delta a}^{(i)}, \Delta a^{(i)}).
\end{equation}
This gradient truncation preserves relative-head sensitivity while training the absolute branch as the robust reference, making cross-space disagreement more discriminative under visual OOD. For CFG training, the absolute head also replaces its quantized condition with learned null tokens on a subset of samples and learns both conditional and marginal waypoint flows.

\subsection{Joint Failure Detection and Recovery}
At deployment, the policy returns both unnormalized action chunks. Let the live pose be $p_t=(x_t,R_t)$ and the pose component of the current absolute waypoint be $\hat{w}_{p,t}=(\hat{x}_t,\hat{R}_t)$. DUET implements the transport map in Eq.~\eqref{eq:transport_map} as
\begin{equation}
    c_t=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
    \begin{bmatrix}
    (\hat{x}_t-x_t)/\sigma_p \\
    \operatorname{Log}(\hat{R}_tR_t^{-1})/\sigma_R
    \end{bmatrix},-1,1\right),
    \label{eq:recovery_vector}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{Log}:SO(3)\rightarrow\mathfrak{so}(3)$ gives the geodesic rotation correction and $\sigma_p,\sigma_R$ convert pose error into action units. The cross-space disagreement is
\begin{equation}
    D_t=\frac{\left\|c_t-
    \operatorname{clip}(\hat{\delta a}_{p,t},-1,1)\right\|_2}{\sqrt{6}}.
    \label{eq:disagreement}
\end{equation}
This is the primary failure signal. We supplement it with a coarse state-distance term that measures normalized violation of the training-state bounds:
\begin{equation}
    S_t =
    \mathrm{mean}\left[
    \frac{\max(s_{\min} - p_t, 0)}{s_{\max}-s_{\min}}
    +
    \frac{\max(p_t - s_{\max}, 0)}{s_{\max}-s_{\min}}
    \right].
\end{equation}
The resulting failure score and recovery weight are
\begin{align}
    q_t &= D_t + \lambda S_t, \\
    \alpha_t &= \sigma(k(q_t-\tau)).
\end{align}
DUET then interpolates between local execution and global recovery while retaining relative gripper control:
\begin{align}
    a_t &= [a_{p,t},a_{g,t}], \\
    a_{p,t} &= (1-\alpha_t)\hat{\delta a}_{p,t} + \alpha_t c_t, \\
    a_{g,t} &= \hat{\delta a}_{g,t},
    \label{eq:fused_action}
\end{align}
where $[\hat{\delta a}_{p,t},\hat{\delta a}_{g,t}]$ is the relative-head command. Thus relative control dominates along the demonstrated support, while a rising failure score shifts $a_p$ toward the absolute recovery direction; control returns smoothly after the two representations realign. When requesting a new chunk, the previous fusion weight also softens the absolute-head CFG scale,
\begin{equation}
    w_t = w_{\max} - (w_{\max}-w_{\min})\alpha_{t-1}.
\end{equation}
The disagreement $D_t$ therefore determines \emph{when} recovery is needed, while the vector $c_t$ determines \emph{how} to recover.

\section{EXPERIMENTS}

\subsection{Experimental Questions and Protocol}
Our experiments answer three questions in order. \textbf{Q1: Nominal capability.} Does the absolute branch preserve standard LIBERO performance relative to established VLA baselines and the relative branch of the same checkpoint? \textbf{Q2: OOD robustness.} Does adaptive dual-action control improve closed-loop completion under behavioral and transient visual shifts? \textbf{Q3: Component necessity.} Which training and inference components are responsible for the gain?

We use the four LIBERO suites \cite{libero}---Spatial, Object, Goal, and LIBERO-10---with $10$ tasks per suite and $50$ initial states per task ($500$ episodes per method per suite). Each DUET suite uses a separate dual-head checkpoint trained for $10$k steps. For OOD experiments, \emph{DUET-Rel} executes only the relative head of that same checkpoint, whereas \emph{DUET} uses adaptive fusion. This is the primary causal comparison: the VLM, training data, checkpoint, task, initial state, and perturbation are held fixed, and only use of the absolute branch changes.

Perturbations are injected at a fixed control step using deterministic event seeds, so DUET and DUET-Rel are paired on identical $(\mathrm{task},\mathrm{init})$ keys. We report task success, paired McNemar $p$-values, and injection coverage. When an event requires a semantic precondition---a grasp for object drop, for example---we additionally report results among episodes in which both methods received the perturbation; untriggered episodes are never silently discarded from the overall rate.

The bound-violation term $S_t$ is almost always zero on LIBERO. For robot-state and world-state shifts we therefore scale disagreement by the residual to the current absolute waypoint,
\begin{equation}
q_t = D_t \cdot \operatorname{clip}(\|c_{t,\mathrm{xyz}}\| / 0.05,\, 0, 1),
\end{equation}
so the gate rises only when disagreement coincides with a meaningful correction distance. Action-execution and contact experiments use the default score $q_t=D_t+\lambda S_t$.

\subsubsection{OOD Taxonomy}
We organize OOD by the locus at which successful execution support is violated (Table~\ref{tab:ood_taxonomy}).

\emph{Behavioral OOD} keeps vision usable but perturbs one of three elements of closed-loop behavior. \textbf{Robot-state shift} changes the robot pose directly. \textbf{World-state shift} changes the manipulated object or grasp while the robot may remain near its old pose. \textbf{Action-execution shift} changes the map from the policy command to the executed command. These loci cover robot state, world state, and the action channel without conflating action corruption with physical robot--world contact.

\emph{Transient visual OOD} instead removes observations for a short horizon. The dual heads must not be queried on black frames; DUET holds the last reliable absolute waypoint until vision returns. Contact force is reported after the behavioral experiments as an additional interaction stress test because it changes contact dynamics but often leaves no persistent waypoint residual.

\paragraph{Robot-State Shift.}
An instantaneous end-effector teleport leaves the arm off the demonstrated chart while objects typically remain in place. The residual-weighted score $q_t=D_t\cdot r_t$, with $r_t=\operatorname{clip}(\|c_{t,\mathrm{xyz}}\|/0.05,0,1)$, is therefore appropriate: the demonstrated grasp target remains valid, so a large waypoint residual should raise the gate rather than be treated as a stale world reference.

Table~\ref{tab:behavioral} shows that DUET raises overall success from $40.0\%$ to $61.7\%$ ($+21.7$~pp over $2000$ paired episodes). The gain is concentrated where a valid grasp target still exists after the jump: Object $+39.8$~pp, LIBERO-10 $+25.0$~pp, Goal $+19.0$~pp. After successful injection, Object recovery is $72.8\%$ for DUET versus $33.2\%$ for DUET-Rel, consistent with a large residual $\|c_t\|$ unblocking disagreement-driven return.

Goal remains weak on opening the top drawer and on pushing a plate ($1$--$3/50$ and $-18$~pp, respectively), i.e., contact-rich primitives whose demonstrated waypoints are poor recovery targets.

\paragraph{World-State Shift.}
The revised grasp-drop protocol models a failed hold rather than a lateral knock. At policy step $67$, if a free-joint object is in gripper contact, the evaluator opens the fingers, preserves object $XY$, lowers the object by $5$~cm in $Z$, and forces the gripper open for $15$ steps. The arm itself is not displaced. If no object is grasped, the event is marked \texttt{not\_grasped} and the simulator is left unchanged.

This protocol tests a different boundary from robot teleport: the robot remains near the demonstrated grasp pose while the manipulated object changes state. In the matched Object run, DUET succeeds on $476/500$ episodes ($95.2\%$) versus $446/500$ ($89.2\%$) for DUET-Rel, a $+6.0$~pp gain (bootstrap 95\% CI $[3.4,8.8]$~pp; McNemar $p{=}2.4{\times}10^{-5}$). Injection succeeds in $498/500$ DUET and $494/500$ DUET-Rel episodes. Figure~\ref{fig:ood_mechanism_grid}(b) shows that $r_t$ rises during the forced-open window while mean $\alpha_t$ remains low: the controller preserves local visual re-acquisition rather than blindly following a potentially stale absolute grasp.

A Goal-suite fixed-step diagnostic further shows why injection coverage must be explicit. At step $67$, DUET and DUET-Rel both receive the vertical drop in only $282/500$ paired episodes because drawer, stove-switch, and rack tasks often have no grasp at that time. Across all episodes the rates are $53.6\%$ and $50.4\%$, respectively; among both-injected pairs they are $23.4\%$ and $17.7\%$. The difference is dominated by one bowl-to-stove task, so we do not treat it as a suite-level World-state result.

\paragraph{Action-Execution Shift.}
Corrupting the $6$D command for a short window drives the arm off the successful trajectory without an explicit pose reset. Unlike teleport, both $D_t$ and $\alpha_t$ rise gradually as execution error accumulates (Fig.~\ref{fig:ood_mechanism_grid}(c)). This is the strongest dual-action setting in Table~\ref{tab:behavioral}: over eight suite$\times$severity groups ($4000$ pairs), DUET attains $64.6\%$ versus $27.9\%$ for DUET-Rel ($+36.7$~pp). Gains grow with severity (Object severe $+52.8$~pp). The response is generated by cross-action disagreement, not an external jump detector.

\subsubsection{Additional Interaction Stress Test}
An external force during contact perturbs robot--world dynamics without explicitly resetting the end-effector pose. Table~\ref{tab:force} reports eight suite$\times$severity groups: DUET reaches $95.95\%$ versus $92.22\%$ for DUET-Rel ($+3.73$~pp). The gain is non-negative in every cell and is largest on Goal ($+7.6$ and $+9.6$~pp), where drawer and plate interactions amplify short contact disturbances. The margin is smaller than for teleport or direct action corruption, consistent with force often producing a shorter-lived residual. We therefore report it separately as an interaction stress test rather than relabeling it as either Robot-state or Action-execution shift.

DUET instead caches the last reliable absolute waypoint and executes its correction with $\alpha{=}1$ for the dropout horizon.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}
Table~\ref{tab:ablations} separates inference-time and training-time ablations. Inference variants reuse the same dual-head checkpoint: DUET-Rel fixes $\alpha=0$, absolute-only fixes $\alpha=1$, fixed fusion sets $\alpha=0.5$, and no adaptive CFG holds the absolute guidance scale at one. These comparisons test whether performance comes from adaptive switching rather than merely adding a second prediction.

Training ablations require independent checkpoints with the same data and optimization budget. RVQ-to-MLP tests discrete manifold anchoring; symmetric augmentation allows the relative head to train on perturbed images; and state-aware ABS gives the absolute branch direct proprioception, testing whether state exclusion prevents a shortcut. No completed runs currently support these rows, so the table records the preregistered comparisons rather than expected outcomes. We evaluate components on clean execution and representative Robot-state and Action-execution shifts; the World-state drop is not used to establish recovery quality because the waypoint itself may be invalid.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}
\textbf{Q1}: on suite-specific $10$k checkpoints, DUET averages $93.8\%$ versus $96.9\%$ for DUET-Rel and $96.9\%$ reported for $\pi_{0.5}$; the clean cost of adaptive fusion is concentrated on Goal and LIBERO-10. \textbf{Q2 is supported across the evaluated shifts}: DUET improves robot-state teleport by $21.7$~pp, vertical World-state drop by $6.0$~pp, action-execution shift by $36.7$~pp, cached visual dropout by $8.8$ to $17.3$~pp, and the separate external-force stress test by $3.73$~pp (Table~\ref{tab:ood_overall}). \textbf{Q3 is only partly answered}: disabling the absolute branch explains the OOD gain, while holding CFG at $w{=}1$ does not change Object clean or action-medium success; absolute-only, fixed-$\alpha$, and all training ablations remain unrun.

\section{LIMITATIONS}

DUET assumes access to absolute state labels aligned with the relative action chunks. This is natural in LIBERO and many robot logs, but may require additional calibration or state estimation on real hardware. The absolute waypoint is an RVQ demonstration anchor, not an object tracker: a World-state drop can invalidate the grasp reference even when the robot remains nearby. The bound-violation term $S_t$ is uninformative on LIBERO, so robot-state gating relies on the residual to the predicted waypoint; this still cannot distinguish ``the arm left a valid target'' from ``the target itself moved.'' Transient visual robustness requires holding the last reliable waypoint rather than running the dual heads on corrupted frames. The matched clean benchmark, the vertical-drop Object rerun, all component ablations, and real-robot evaluation are not yet complete in this draft.

\section{CONCLUSION}

We presented DUET, a dual-branch VLA policy for endogenous failure detection and recovery. By aligning a local relative-action tangent field with a stateless, RVQ-anchored absolute-waypoint manifold, DUET uses cross-space disagreement to detect when execution becomes OOD and to activate a closed-loop recovery direction. This unifies failure detection and recovery without an external detector or additional recovery demonstrations.
